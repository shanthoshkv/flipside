The desk is the button

Open the app and it is already listening. The first launch asks for one subject and a daily goal. Physics, organic, whatever you are actually studying. Lay the phone screen-down on the desk. After it has been down for a second, the session starts.

The screen goes almost black. The time stays on it, in dim type, so the phone is not a lamp on the desk. Press the phone at the top of this page and you get that screen.

Pick it up and hold it for a little over half a second. That ends the session. The wait is there so a knock on the table does not throw away an hour. If it was down for less than 10 seconds, nothing is saved. You changed your mind, and the log does not need that.

There is a Start button in Settings if flipping feels wrong, and a pause option. Lift to pause, put it down again to continue. Phones do not all sit the same way. Sensitivity is Low, Medium or High. Low wants the phone flat and still. High starts and stops more easily. You can also calibrate: three seconds face-down on the desk you actually use, then three seconds held the way you hold it when you check the time. After that, the Low, Medium and High choice is left alone.

The clock is not a counter the app adds to. It stores the start time and subtracts. Every 15 seconds it writes the time down. If the battery dies, or Android closes the app, the next launch ends the session at that last note. Locking the screen does not stop it. Leaving the app does not stop it. A foreground notification keeps the sensor awake. You can pause that listening when you want the battery, and turn it back on when you sit down.

Three ways to count, one way to stop

Stopwatch, Pomodoro, or Flowtime. You pick before you flip. Putting the phone down still starts it, and picking it up still stops it.

Stopwatch counts up until you lift the phone.

Pomodoro counts down. The lengths in the app are 25/5, 50/10, 52/17 and 90/20. A short beep marks the end of a block. Break time is stored as a break, so five minutes of standing up does not turn into five more minutes of physics.

Flowtime has no length. You work until you stop. The app then suggests a break of about a fifth of that time, at least 5 minutes and at most 25.

When a session ends, the app asks how it felt. Rough, Meh, Okay, Good, or Deep. You can add a line about what you covered, or skip it. If you forgot the phone and used a kitchen timer, open the day and add the session by hand. It is marked as added by hand, so you can tell later.

A day of ten-minute blocks

The Plan tab is the whole day, six blocks to an hour. A hollow block is what you meant to study. A solid block is what the timer recorded, in that subject's colour. If you planned it and also did it, the block is solid with a thin mark across the top.

Tap a block, or drag across a row, to paint with the subject you have selected. Dragging over a block of that same subject rubs it out. Recorded time stays. You cannot erase an hour you already sat by painting over it.

The screen in the app runs from midnight to midnight, and you can move through two weeks. The week can start on Monday or Sunday. This example is 2pm to 10pm, so it fits on the page. The default planning window is 5pm to 10pm, in blocks of 50 minutes. Both can be changed.

Auto-plan is for the days you will not paint by hand. It looks at your exams and topics, puts the nearest exam first, and mixes subjects so one of them does not eat the whole evening. It skips time you already planned, and today it starts at the next free slot, not at midnight you have already missed. The button here is a smaller version of that. It fills free time from 5 to 10 with these three subjects, in 50-minute blocks. It does not know about your exams.

Subjects, exams, and the cards

A subject has a colour, a weekly target in minutes, and topics under it. Tick a topic when you have covered it. That tick is what the coverage number is made of. Archive a subject when the course is over. It leaves the lists, and the old sessions stay in the stats.

An exam is a name, a date, and a subject. The home screen shows the next one, how many days are left, and how many of that subject's topics are done. Under a week, the number is shown in the warning colour. The same block can sit on your home screen as a widget.

Flashcards are separate from the timer. In Settings you pick FSRS or SM-2. FSRS schedules the next review from how hard the card felt, and aims at a recall rate you set. The default is 90%. SM-2 is the older method, still there if you already know it. New cards and reviews are capped per day, so a deck of 2,000 cards does not become the whole night. The defaults are 20 new cards and 200 reviews.

A card can be a question, a sentence with a blank, the same fact turned around so the answer is the question, or a picture from your gallery. You can paste a pile of cards from text instead of typing them one by one. A widget tells you how many are due, and opens the review.

Where the hours went

The charts are drawn in the app, as SVG, not pulled in from a chart library. A week of bars, with your daily goal as a thin line. Time by subject. Which hours of the day you actually work. A year of small squares. A streak.

The streak can ignore one empty day each week. A day off does not reset you to zero. Today does not break it either, because the day is not over. The switch is in Settings, and it starts on.

Tap a day and you get the sessions, the rating, the note, and the three diary lines. You can edit a session or add one there. Once a week, if you want, the app adds the week up, checks the target on each subject, and leaves a plain note about next week.

Sounds, widgets, and the rest

Eight sounds ship inside the app. Rain, showers, ocean, a stream, forest, a campfire, a fan, and white noise. They are short recordings, cut so they loop. Nothing is streamed. The app is not allowed on the internet, so it could not stream them if it wanted to. The buttons here are a rough synthesised stand-in, so you can hear the idea. The files in the app sound better. Volume has its own slider.

Do Not Disturb can turn on with a session and go back to how you had it when you stop. Android asks for permission to change notification policy. You can say no. The timer still works. A Pomodoro can also notify you when a block ends. That needs notification permission too. Reminders before a planned block exist, and they stay off until you turn them on. Quiet hours default to 10pm until 7am.

There are six home screen widgets. Today is a ring against the daily goal, plus the streak. Next exam is the days left. Quick start opens the timer on one subject. Week is seven bars. Cards due opens the review. Subjects shows three of them, and tapping one starts that subject. A shortcut can do the same job with a link like flipside://timer?task=12.

The widgets stay dark, and they stay amber, even if you change the accent inside the app. The rest of the app follows what you pick. Amber, ocean, mint, rose, or violet. Theme is dark, light, or match the phone. Text size runs from small to huge. Fonts include this one, JetBrains Mono, plus Atkinson Hyperlegible and Lexend if you want something easier to read, a serif, a rounded face, and a pixel font. The accent colours are checked for contrast in the test suite.

A backup is a JSON file in a folder you pick. The app writes one about once a day and keeps the newest seven. Restore is in Settings. The file has tasks, sessions, plans, the diary, exams, cards, reviews, and settings. On a new phone, copy the file over, restore, then choose the backup folder again. The old path will not exist.

The interface is in English, Hindi, Spanish, Portuguese, Indonesian, French, and German. It follows the phone language unless you pick one. The English sentence is the key in the translation table, so a fix is a straight edit.

It has no way to send your data anywhere

The release build does not ask for the internet permission. Android will not let it open a connection. There is no account. There are no ads. Nothing is counted for analytics.

Sessions live in a SQLite database on the phone. You can delete all of it from Settings. Backups go only to the folder you chose. The source is public, under the MIT licence, if you want to read the permission list yourself. The permissions it does ask for are the foreground service that keeps flip detection alive, notifications, vibration, and, only if you turn it on, access to Do Not Disturb.

Installing

Download FlipSide.apk on the phone. Open the file. If Android asks, allow install from this source. If an older copy is already installed, uninstall that one first. The new file may refuse to replace it.

Download FlipSide.apk

You can also build it. Expo Go is not enough. The widgets, the service that keeps the sensor alive, and Do Not Disturb are native code.

npm install
npx tsc --noEmit
npm run check
npx eas-cli build -p android --profile preview

npm run check covers the flip detector, the card scheduler, the planner, the stats, reminders, contrast, and the translations. With Android Studio installed you can also prebuild and assemble a release locally.

It is Expo SDK 57, TypeScript, and a short Kotlin module for Do Not Disturb. If you want to help, translations are the easiest place. They live in src/locales. Bug reports and changes are welcome on GitHub.
